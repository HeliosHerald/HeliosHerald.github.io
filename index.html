About

Transforming art from something we see to something we do.

I’m a second-year MA student in Arts Management at American University, where I am co-president of the Emerging Arts Leaders Society. I’m Program Coordinator at the DC Arts Center in Adams Morgan, where I built and run the AdMo Pen Pals Project.

What I believe

I believe that one day, when our brighter future comes to pass, most Americans will be proud artists. We will show new friends elaborate photo albums; we will mourn our pets through written poetry and song. We will paint, staple, sew, and sing so that we might know a more harmonious country.

Today, fewer than one in five Americans practice art in some meaningful way1. I want to change that. The barrier to artistic creation has never been lower, the benefits of arts engagement have never been more proven, human authorship of art has never been more endangered.

Literacy was once a rare skill possessed by only the rich and privileged—until it was deemed critical social and civic infrastructure. Art must follow a similar trajectory. We must demand art education be a public good, not a private luxury.

All humans deserve a space to learn, make, and share art.

What I'm looking for

I’m an emerging arts leader—I need allies.

I’d love to hear from potential mentors, collaborators, and practicing arts managers. Tell me: how can I maximize my employability in this field and gather the resources to help invigorate arts and culture?

2026 – present Program DC Arts Center, AdMo

AdMo Pen Pals

Creating connection through written correspondence.

A neighborhood pen pal program I built at the DC Arts Center to bring back the lost art of correspondence. Neighbors sign up, receive a handcrafted welcome letter, and get matched with someone nearby—80+ people so far, plus a second chapter now running at the University of Florida.

  • 80+ sign-ups
  • Two ongoing programs
  • Dozens of letters delivered
  • A replicable matching framework
The catalyst question

How can I connect with my neighbors through writing?

Sleepless nights tend to poke and prod at our poor weary brains. Usually all that poking is unproductive, just to exhaust the poor organ into dreamy submission. Sometimes, there is an exception. I’m not sure how many pokes it took, but eventually an idea shot out from my unconscious and eclipsed all other thoughts: I wanted a pen pal. I desperately wanted to write a letter to someone nearby with the guarantee that they would read it. This used to be easy, now it is hard. I really wanted to make it easy again—not just for me, but for everyone.

The DC Arts Center Pen Pal Project is a local pen pal initiative that seeks to keep the art of correspondence alive by creating and sustaining friendships and dialogue in Adams Morgan. The local aspect is crucial; all participants have a relationship with this neighborhood. Most modes of correspondence today encourage distance—both personal and physical. The pen pal project closes that distance. Distance is not necessarily bad, but we have enough of it. It’s time for something that asks us to be earnest, to be real.

Writing pen-to-paper encourages people to think differently, to share sincerely, to be intentional. To connect with a stranger and wait patiently for a response—that is rare too. The benefits of having a pen pal have long been known, but opportunities to participate are infrequent.

Thanks to the institutional support of DCAC, finding a pen pal is easy. Users sign up with a brief survey and soon after are notified of a welcome letter awaiting them at DCAC. The welcome letter is the key to their correspondence; within it they learn how the system functions and—most importantly—who their pen pal is. Every aspect of the experience is accessible: envelopes are provided, and all writing levels are encouraged.

Sincere correspondence builds community. The DCAC Pen Pal Project endeavors to create a more connected neighborhood through the shared joy of letter writing.

The project is a replicable framework. It is now established at my alma mater, the University of Florida, under a club I used to lead: The English Society. Students can send and retrieve letters straight from the university library.

Rove 54

Uncover the six seals, become a rover.

A guerrilla scavenger hunt across Adams Morgan, powered by hidden NFC stickers and Arthurian myth. Participants follow riddles to six secret seals, collect community-authored proverbs, and earn copper artifacts—all coordinated through a site I built at rove54.quest.

  • A working site at rove54.quest
  • NFC technology
  • Copper artifacts
  • Hidden cache
  • Community-sourced proverbs
  • Six hidden clues
  • The roundtable, renewed
The catalyst question

How can I bring the mythos of the knight’s roundtable to my neighborhood?

In a moment of intense hardship, head in my hands, I wondered: how did the knights of yore find their strength? Belief in divinity helped, but there was another thing: fellowship. They shared burdens, wisdom, meals and ordeals. I saw genuine value in that ancient framework, and besides, knights are awesome. I resolved to build a quest that encourages collaboration and connection culminating in a roundtable seminar.

I knew that the core model would be that of a scavenger hunt around the neighborhood. I took a walk around AdMo and scouted six locations—spots where I could hide a sticker. After that, a flurry of iteration and experimentation had begun. I had decided that I wanted NFC technology to be the engine of the hunt—the same technology behind tap-to-pay. NFC stickers are very cheap, and when tapped with a phone they can transfer a link, just like a QR code. Stickers plus NFC created a discreet way of adhering interactable clues to walls.

Unbeknownst to me, this decision would have ripple effects that demanded a much larger scope. I had to create a website to host all the necessary information and cache clue activations; I had to determine how to best teach users about the NFC functionality. I wanted it to feel like magic, but magic cannot be explained. Striking the balance between mysterious scavenger hunt and accessible public program was an ongoing challenge. I had to wear three hats: public programmer, game designer, and the heavy steel helm of a knight.

There was a fourth hat too: graphic designer. I wanted Rove 54 to have a distinct aesthetic identity, a style that carried its Arthurian inspiration but was unmistakably contemporary. I commissioned my friend and talented artist @minivan.studio to design the Rove 54 sigil. That design became the keystone for the project’s aesthetic identity, and following his contribution I created a flyer and two copper artifacts, rewards for completing the quest.

The Pledge maps the six seal locations, but it also does something much more significant: it commemorates previous Rovers. Each Rove 54 clue webpage carries a proverb, a short wisdom written by a previous Rover. Since this was my first experiment, there weren’t any previous Rovers yet, so I called on my friends. They may not have completed Rove 54 personally, but they’ve each inspired and supported me. They embody the spirit of Rove 54, and that makes them honorary Rovers. The Pledge distills what I believe is the core value of the project: everyone carries lived wisdom, and we all may exist in community and share our challenges with one another.

The other copper artifact is the coin. Admittedly, the design is a little rough, but I stand by the idea. Since Rove 54 is accessed through an NFC tag, access to the webpage can be discreetly hidden in a coin. The coin becomes a peer sponsorship mechanism: a rover completes the hunt, gets a coin, and gives that coin to a friend to challenge them to the rove.

I beta tested Rove 54 and those who participated enjoyed the experience. I got valuable feedback and was able to tighten all the loose screws on the project.

The High Dive

Street interviews on carbon copy paper.

A street-interview zine that stops strangers with a single question about an object they’re carrying. Each encounter produces both a carbon-copy keepsake for the participant and archival material for the zine.

The catalyst question

How can I talk to a total stranger and solicit a worthwhile, brief, and sentimental encounter?

For a long time I wanted to make a zine, but I wasn’t interested in producing something that only platformed my voice. I sat on this for a while before a different idea came to me: a system for performing memorable street interviews with strangers. The product of those interviews would become the foundation for the zine, and various fictional voices—written by me—would become the other half of each spread.

The process is straightforward but unique: I approach a stranger and ask them if they’d be interested in a one-question interview for the High Dive. If they say yes, I begin by asking them to choose an object from the bag to be the object of their interview. Sometimes I help them decide what object to talk about.

We begin with an informal conversation about the object and its meaning to the speaker. Then, I hand them a sheet of paper with a prompt: if this object was on display in a museum, how would you label it?

They write their response and then hand it back to me. Then, I reveal the surprise: through the power of carbon copy paper, they get a stylized copy of their response to take home.

I take the drab original copy home to scan and include in the High Dive. At first I intended for the High Dive to be almost entirely composed of these interviews, but I felt like they needed a reason to exist—something more. Most editorial techniques would distract or diminish from the original interviews, but I had an idea: what if my written contributions were characters of their own, and what if they responded to the interview in their own way? From there, everything fell into place.

I hope to publish the first issue in late 2026 / early 2027!

How this site was made

This is the personal site of Tristan Krammel, an arts management student and cultural animator in Washington, DC.

Design & writing. Every word, every design decision, and every idea here is my own — the concept, the structure, the illustrations and textures, the writing, and the game on the Patio.

Built with. HTML, CSS, and JavaScript hosted on GitHub Pages. Type is set in Fraunces and Karla. The ivy, the tab icons, and the clay-tile and contour textures were drawn for this site by AI.

A note on AI. I built this site in close collaboration with an AI assistant (Clayde), which wrote the code. The vision, the words, and the design direction are mine. I am emphatically against the use of generative AI for art and expression; all use of AI to produce this website was considered and measured in accordance with my values.

Thanks. The Rove 54 sigil was designed by @minivan.studio.
